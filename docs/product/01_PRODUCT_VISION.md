# 01 — Product Vision

> **2.0 revision:** `docs/design/DIG_DOWN_2_0_DESIGN.md` extends this vision with positive discovery rewards, light production, shared colony capabilities and logistics that prompt replanning. The fantasy remains an autonomous mouse colony building a home.

**Status: LOCKED concept / PROVISIONAL tuning**

## 1. Working title
**Dig Down — 地下鼠鼠**

## 2. One-sentence pitch
Lead a small mouse colony through a freely excavated underground cross-section where every promising signal, accidental flood, blocked route, and discovered cavity permanently reshapes the home you are trying to build.

## 3. Player fantasy
The player is not an omnipotent architect and not a single adventurer. The player acts as the colony's spatial judgment:
- where to excavate;
- where to stop;
- what space should be used for;
- which danger is worth approaching;
- how to turn consequences into useful parts of the burrow.

Mice perform the physical labor and visibly live inside the result.

## 4. Core emotional arc
The target feeling is not constant danger. It cycles:
1. **Curiosity** — “What is behind this soil?”
2. **Commitment** — “I am going to dig toward that clue.”
3. **Surprise** — “That changed more than I expected.”
4. **Scramble** — mice react, move, repair, reroute.
5. **Mastery** — the player understands and adapts.
6. **Belonging** — the changed area becomes part of home.

The final emotional payoff is seeing a strange, imperfect burrow whose form records the player's history.

## 5. Product pillars

### Pillar A — Free excavation, meaningful terrain
The player can dig freely. The world does not force route-choice prompts. Terrain is deliberately unequal so freedom creates real decisions.

### Pillar B — Read the underground
Unknown space leaks clues. Soil moisture, roots, cracks, warmth, sound, and debris allow inference without removing uncertainty.

### Pillar C — Consequences become architecture
Discoveries do not merely grant loot. They change pathing, room suitability, local safety, or environment states.

### Pillar D — Autonomous cozy colony
Mice make the burrow feel alive by working, hauling, eating, resting, reacting, and furnishing zones. The player manages space instead of micromanaging bodies.

### Pillar E — Systemic stories at small scale
Memorable stories emerge from spatial interactions: a water breach floods food storage; a new drain trench saves the colony; the flooded zone later becomes a mushroom/wet utility area.

## 6. Genre position
Primary:
- 2D systemic sandbox
- underground spatial exploration
- light colony simulation

Secondary:
- cozy management
- procedural exploration
- environmental puzzle without fixed solutions

Not positioned as:
- combat survival game
- mining economy game
- crafting sandbox
- hardcore colony simulator
- narrative choice game

## 7. Target session pattern
The game should support both:
- **10–20 minute sessions:** inspect the frontier, commit one excavation plan, resolve a discovery, leave the colony stable.
- **45–90 minute sessions:** extend deeper, reorganize multiple rooms, complete a chapter objective.

## 8. Target audience
Players who enjoy:
- cozy/systemic games;
- seeing autonomous creatures work;
- transforming environments;
- light planning without spreadsheet management;
- procedural worlds that remain legible;
- “small mistake becomes a story” moments.

## 9. Signature GIF
A mouse digs one suspicious damp tile. Water breaches into an existing corridor. Several mice rush to move food while the player opens a lower trench and seals a doorway. Later, the same flooded cavity is a stable water/wet-use zone with mice calmly passing nearby.

The viewer should understand the game without explanation:
> “You dig a living underground home, and what you uncover changes it.”

## 10. Differentiation
### From Terraria/Core Keeper
Less combat/crafting; more consequence to colony topology and room suitability.

### From Oxygen Not Included
Much simpler simulation; stronger focus on excavation choice and readable environmental effects.

### From RimWorld
Far less character simulation; stories emerge primarily from spatial consequences rather than storyteller events.

### From SteamWorld Dig
Less character-platforming; excavation affects a persistent autonomous settlement.

## 11. Success metrics for design
The game is on target if playtests show:
- players frequently pause at the frontier before issuing digs;
- players articulate predictions from environmental signals;
- different players produce visibly different burrow structures from the same broad chapter goals;
- accidents prompt replanning more often than reloads;
- players can explain why a strange room or corridor exists;
- the colony is understandable without managing each mouse.

## 12. Failure modes
The vision has failed if:
- players simply strip-mine every tile;
- unknown content feels like arbitrary RNG;
- hazards are solved by one generic repair button;
- room layouts converge on one optimal rectangle;
- mouse management becomes the dominant attention sink;
- new chapters only add reskinned ore/resources;
- the project requires realistic simulation to remain interesting.
